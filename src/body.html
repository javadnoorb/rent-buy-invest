Net worth over time

Liquidation value if you sold everything at the end of each year. Dots mark refinancings.

How to read this
What you see
x: years from today. y: what each household would have if it sold everything that year, after the sale commission, paying off the mortgage, and taxes. Shaded bands, when shown, hold the middle 50% of simulated futures.
What to look for
Where the green (buy) line crosses the blue (rent + invest) line, marked by the dashed vertical line, is the break-even year. The vertical gap at your horizon is the dollar answer. If the green and blue bands overlap a lot, the outcome is uncertain even though the lines differ.
Keep in mind
The lines assume your growth rates hold exactly every year. The buyer starts lower because closing costs and the eventual selling costs are lost up front.

Where the gap comes from

The difference between buying and renting at your horizon, split into what adds to it (green, in buying's favor) and what takes from it (blue, in renting's favor). The parts add up exactly to the answer above.

How to read this
What you see
Each arrow starts where the previous one ended and points right when the part favors buying, left when it favors renting, so the arrows walk from zero to the final gap at the bottom. Both households spend the same each month; whoever pays less invests the difference.
The parts
Home price change, selling costs and any tax on the sale are what the house itself returns. Buying costs, mortgage interest, property tax, insurance and upkeep are what owning costs. Tax savings and house-hack income offset them. Rent is what the renter pays instead. The renter's investment returns are what the down payment and the monthly differences earned, net of the buyer's own investments and of taxes on both portfolios.
What to look for
Usually the race is between the home price change and the renter's investment returns, with interest and rent the largest costs on each side. Principal payments don't appear: they move money from cash into the house without changing net worth.

Monthly outlay

Owner's cost by component (stacked) against rent. Whoever pays less each month invests the difference. Tax savings are credited once a year.

How to read this
What you see
x: year. y: dollars per month. Stacked bars above zero are what the owner pays, by component; the bar below zero is the tax saving. The blue line is the renter's cost (rent, renter's insurance and expected moving costs).
What to look for
Principal (green) grows and interest (red) shrinks over time; that shift is equity building. Where the blue line rises above the top of the stack, owning becomes the cheaper month and the owner starts investing the difference.
Keep in mind
Principal is not money lost; it comes back when you sell. The next chart leaves it out to show the true cost.

Unrecoverable cost per year

Money that builds no wealth: interest, property tax, insurance, upkeep, HOA, PMI, refinancing, minus tax savings, plus the forgone portfolio return on home equity. Compared with rent.

How to read this
What you see
x: year. y: dollars per year. Dark green is cash the owner never gets back: interest, property tax, insurance, upkeep, HOA, PMI, utilities and refinance costs, minus tax savings. Light green is what the owner's equity would have earned invested (opportunity cost). The blue line is the renter's yearly cost.
What to look for
If the green stack is below the blue line, owning is cheaper that year before counting any price growth. If it is above, home appreciation has to make up the difference. A common rule of thumb puts this unrecoverable cost near 5% of the home's value per year.
Keep in mind
Appreciation, the owner's main gain, is deliberately excluded here. Compare the gap with what you expect the house to gain.

Where the buyer's wealth sits

Home equity net of selling costs and tax, plus the buyer's own portfolio.

How to read this
What you see
x: years. y: dollars. The green area is what the house gives you if sold (value minus selling costs, mortgage and tax). The blue area on top is the buyer's own investments. The dotted line is the home's market value; the dashed red line is the remaining mortgage.
What to look for
How much of the buyer's wealth is tied up in one house rather than in diversified investments. The gap between the dotted line and the top of the green area is the mortgage balance plus what selling costs (commission, fees and tax).
Keep in mind
Home equity can't be spent without selling or borrowing against it.

Liquidity cushion

Assets you could reach without selling the house, in months of the household budget (the larger of owning and renting costs).

How to read this
What you see
x: years. y: months of household costs that liquid assets could cover, on a log scale. Red dotted lines mark 3 and 6 months, the usual emergency-fund guidance.
What to look for
Whether the buyer stays below 3 months for several years. That is when a job loss or a big repair would force borrowing or a sale. The renter is usually far above the lines because nothing is locked in a house.
Keep in mind
Assumes neither household keeps a separate emergency fund. A home-equity line of credit can help the buyer, but lenders cut those lines in downturns.

What moves the answer

Change in the final advantage of buying over renting and investing when each input moves by the stated amount, all else fixed. Bars right of zero favor buying.

How to read this
What you see
Each row is one input. The bars show how much the final buy-minus-rent gap changes if that input goes up (green) or down (blue) by the stated amount. The axis title shows the starting gap.
What to look for
The longest bars are the assumptions that matter most, so check those against real quotes and your own judgment. If a bar is longer than the starting gap and points the other way, that input alone can flip the decision.
Keep in mind
Inputs move one at a time here. In reality some move together; for example, high inflation tends to raise rents, home prices and mortgage rates at once.

Home appreciation × portfolio return

Final advantage of buying (% of today's price) across nominal growth rates. Black contour: break-even. Marker: your assumption.

How to read this
What you see
x: yearly home appreciation. y: yearly portfolio return before fees. Color: final buy advantage as a % of today's price (green, buying wins; blue, renting wins). The black line is a tie, and ✕ marks your assumptions.
What to look for
How far ✕ sits from the black line is your margin of safety. The slope of the line tells you how many points of extra home appreciation offset one point of extra stock return. Leverage makes home appreciation count for more, so the line is usually steeper than 45°: a small rise in home appreciation offsets a large rise in portfolio return.
Keep in mind
All other inputs are held at your settings.

Break-even year vs. rent

Advantage of buying at each horizon for different rents, holding the price fixed.

How to read this
What you see
x: years. y: buy minus (rent + invest). Each line uses a different rent for the same home, labeled with its price-to-rent ratio (price ÷ yearly rent). The thick grey line is your rent.
What to look for
Where each line crosses zero is the break-even year at that rent. Lower price-to-rent means buying pays off sooner. A common rule of thumb is that ratios under about 15 favor buying and over about 20 favor renting.
Keep in mind
This holds the home price fixed. Use the rent of a truly comparable home, since rents for smaller units make renting look better than it is.

How much house can you afford?

The highest price your income and cash allow under common lending limits, at your mortgage rate, term, loan type, property tax, insurance and HOA. Lenders count principal, interest, tax, insurance, HOA and mortgage insurance; the full monthly cost adds upkeep and utilities. Income and other debts come from the settings (Income & taxes).

Best down payment and term

Buy minus (rent + invest) for every down payment and horizon, with the renter investing the same cash. 15-year loans use the 15-year rate in Home & mortgage.

How to read this
What you see
x: down payment. y: how long you stay. Color: buy advantage as a % of price. Black line: tie. Dots: the best down payment at each horizon.
What to look for
Whether colors get greener to the right, meaning a bigger down payment helps buying. That happens when the mortgage rate is above what the portfolio earns after tax. Below 20% down, mortgage insurance (PMI) adds cost.
Keep in mind
The renter invests the same cash, so a bigger down payment also gives the renter more to invest.

If you had made this decision in the past

Your price, rent and cost settings replayed through actual historical sequences of home prices, rents, stock, bond and bill returns, inflation and mortgage rates, including refinancing if enabled. Always in today's dollars (adjusted with actual inflation); the Today's $ / Nominal $ switch doesn't apply to these replays.

How to read this
What you see
x: years after buying. y: buy minus (rent + invest), after inflation, as a % of the price. Each thin line is one historical start date (blue early, red recent); the thick line is the median.
What to look for
The spread of lines at your horizon shows how much timing and luck matter. The share of lines above zero is how often buying won historically.
Keep in mind
Your price and rent are scaled through each past period's actual growth. Neighboring start years share most of their history, so they aren't independent trials.

Monte Carlo from history

Block bootstrap of joint annual draws (home, rent, stocks, bonds, bills, inflation and mortgage-rate changes from the same years, so their correlations are kept). Re-centering shifts each growth series in log space so its geometric mean equals your assumption while keeping historical volatility and correlation.

Distribution of net worth

Median line, 25–75% band (dark) and 5–95% band (light).

How to read this
What you see
x: years. y: net worth. Lines are the median simulated future; the dark band holds the middle 50%, the light band the middle 90%.
What to look for
How much the green and blue bands overlap. Heavy overlap means the outcome is uncertain. The buying band is usually wider because the house is bought with borrowed money and is a single, undiversified asset.
Keep in mind
Futures are resampled from history and, when re-centering is on, shifted to match your growth assumptions. The tails reflect real historical extremes.

Risk-adjusted comparison

How to read this

The certainty equivalent is the sure amount you would accept instead of the gamble, computed as \(CE = \big(\mathbb{E}[W^{1-\gamma}]\big)^{1/(1-\gamma)}\), where \(W\) is net worth at your horizon in each simulated future, \(\mathbb{E}\) averages across futures, and \(\gamma\) is your risk aversion (set under Life & risk preferences). With \(\gamma = 0\) it equals the average; higher \(\gamma\) weights bad outcomes more. Expected shortfall is the average of the worst 5% of outcomes. The liquidity row is the share of paths where liquid assets fell below 3 months of costs at any year-end in the first 5 years.

Probability buying wins

Share of paths where buying beats each alternative, at each horizon, and with random moving.

How to read this
What you see
x: how long you stay. y: chance buying ends ahead of renting and investing (blue) or of renting with T-bills (gold). The diamond includes a random chance of moving early.
What to look for
Where the blue line crosses 50% is the stay length after which buying wins more often than not. A diamond below the blue line shows how much the chance of an early move hurts buying.
Keep in mind
Winning by $1 and winning by $100k count the same here; use the histogram next to it for size.

Final advantage of buying

How to read this
What you see
x: buy minus (rent + invest) at your horizon. y: number of simulated futures. Green bars: buying won; blue: renting won. The black line is zero.
What to look for
Where most of the mass sits relative to zero, and the shape of the tails. A long left tail means buying occasionally goes badly wrong, for example a price drop just when you sell.
Keep in mind
The median is a more typical outcome than the mean when the tails are long.

What you see

How to read this
What you see
x: years. y: the simulated market 30-year rate; median line and 5–95% band.

How often rates fall far enough below yours to refinance. Lower future rates help the buyer.

Compare locations

Home & rent growth:

Tap a column to sort. Tick up to 6 locations for the chart below.

How to read this
The table
Each row runs the full model for one location with your household. The last column is the headline answer there: positive (green) means buying ends ahead of renting and investing after your horizon. Price ÷ yearly rent is the classic rule of thumb: above about 20 renting tends to win, below about 15 buying does.
Growth modes
Same: your home and rent growth for every location, so differences come only from today's prices, rents and local costs. Yours + local: your growth plus how much faster or slower each location grew than the U.S. over the same years (FHFA home prices since about 1991, BLS rents). Local history: each location's own historical averages. Past local booms need not repeat, so treat the last two as what-ifs.
Housing ÷ income
Month-1 owning cost over your gross monthly income. Green up to 28%, amber to 36%, red above: the same household may not qualify for a mortgage everywhere.
Taxes and what's left out
State income tax follows each location's state unless you enter it manually; the United States row has no state, so it keeps your current state-tax settings. County-wide income and transfer taxes are included where the state rules list them; most city-only taxes and local homestead exemptions are not. Local pay differences are not modeled.

Cumulative real growth, 1928–2025

$1 at the end of 1927, deflated by CPI-U, log scale. Stocks include dividends; the home index is price only.

How to read this
What you see
y: what $1 invested at the end of 1927 grew to, after inflation, on a log scale. Equal vertical distances mean equal percentage growth.
What to look for
Stocks grow far faster but fall hard (1929, 1973, 2000, 2008). Home prices barely beat inflation over long stretches.
Keep in mind
The home line is price only. Owning also saves you rent, which is the main return on a home and why this app models rent explicitly.

Annualized, ending in the year shown.

How to read this
What you see

What to look for

Correlation of annual changes

Pearson correlation, 1928–2025 (mortgage-rate changes 1973–2025).

How to read this
What you see
Pearson correlation of yearly changes: +1 moves together, −1 opposite, 0 unrelated.
What to look for
Low correlation between homes and stocks means each diversifies the other. Positive home–inflation correlation makes housing a partial inflation hedge. Rate rises and bond returns move opposite.
Keep in mind
Correlations shift across eras; these are averages over 1928–2025.

Mortgage rates vs. inflation

Annual average 30-year fixed rate and December-to-December CPI-U inflation.

How to read this
What you see
Red: average 30-year mortgage rate. Gold: inflation. Dotted: real rate (mortgage rate minus inflation). Dashed line: today's rate.
What to look for
Where today's rate sits in history. The real rate is the true cost of borrowing after inflation; it was briefly negative in the mid- and late 1970s and in 2021–22, which favored borrowers.
Keep in mind
A fixed-rate loan locks the nominal rate; higher-than-expected inflation helps the borrower.

Annual growth of CPI rent (all existing leases) vs. Zillow's asking-rent index (new leases).

How to read this
What you see
Blue bars: yearly growth of asking rents on new leases (Zillow). Red line: CPI rent, which averages all existing leases.
What to look for
Asking rents tend to lead CPI rent, so a renter who moves feels market jumps sooner than the index shows.

Zillow values here are January-to-January changes.
