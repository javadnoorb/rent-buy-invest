Net worth over time

Liquidation value if you sold everything at the end of each year. Dots mark refinancings.

How to read this

Each line is what that household would have after selling the house (paying commission and any capital-gains tax), paying off the mortgage, and cashing out its portfolio (paying capital-gains tax). The buyer starts below the renter because buying costs closing fees up front and selling costs at exit. The buyer catches up through principal paydown, appreciation on a leveraged asset, and, once owning gets cheaper than rent, investing the monthly savings. The dashed line marks the year the buyer pulls ahead for good.

Monthly outlay

Owner's cost by component (stacked) against rent. Whoever pays less each month invests the difference. Tax savings are credited once a year.

How to read this

Bars above zero are what the owner pays each month, averaged over the year; the green bar below zero is the federal tax saving from itemizing. Principal is a cost in cash but builds equity. When the blue rent line rises above the bar stack, the owner starts investing the difference.

Unrecoverable cost per year

Money that builds no wealth: interest, property tax, insurance, upkeep, HOA, PMI, refinancing, minus tax savings, plus the forgone portfolio return on home equity. Compared with rent.

How to read this

This is the fairest single-year comparison of owning and renting. Principal is excluded because it stays your money. The forgone return is what your equity would have earned in the portfolio. If the green stack is below the rent line, owning is cheaper that year, before counting appreciation.

Where the buyer's wealth sits

Home equity net of selling costs and tax, plus the buyer's own portfolio.

Liquidity cushion

Assets you could reach without selling the house, in months of the household budget (the larger of owning and renting costs).

How to read this

The renter keeps everything liquid. The buyer's wealth is locked in the house until owning becomes cheaper than renting and surplus starts accumulating. A buyer with fewer than about 3–6 months of costs in liquid assets is exposed if income drops or a big repair comes up. This chart assumes no separate emergency fund on either side.

Home appreciation × portfolio return

Final advantage of buying (% of today's price) across nominal growth rates. Black contour: break-even. Marker: your assumption.

How to read this

Everything above and left of the black line favors renting and investing; below and right favors buying. The slope of the line tells you how many points of extra home appreciation compensate one point of extra stock return. Because the home is bought with leverage, the line is usually flatter than 45°.

Risk-adjusted comparison

How to read this

The certainty equivalent is the sure amount you would accept instead of the gamble, for a person with constant relative risk aversion \(\gamma\): \(CE = \big(\mathbb{E}[W^{1-\gamma}]\big)^{1/(1-\gamma)}\). With \(\gamma = 0\) it equals the mean; higher \(\gamma\) weights bad outcomes more. Expected shortfall is the average of the worst 5% of outcomes. The liquidity row is the share of paths where liquid assets fell below 3 months of costs at any year-end in the first 5 years.
